About COAST

South Florida sits at the leading edge of coupled coastal Earth-system change: intensifying tropical cyclones, marine heatwaves, accelerating sea-level rise, reef decline, and coastal solid-Earth responses to ocean and storm forcing. COAST uses this region and the broader tropical western Atlantic as a living laboratory for undergraduate research that crosses traditional disciplinary boundaries.

The Rosenstiel School is one of very few places in the United States where atmospheric, ocean, marine-biology, environmental, and geoscience faculty work side by side on Virginia Key. The school houses the NOAA Cooperative Institute for Marine and Atmospheric Studies (CIMAS), sits directly across the street from NOAA's Atlantic Oceanographic and Meteorological Laboratory (AOML), and is near the U.S. National Hurricane Center. COAST embeds students in this research environment for a full ten weeks of mentored, hands-on coastal Earth-system science.

Observation & Analysis

Field, laboratory, and data

Students work with field observations, autonomous platforms, satellite remote sensing, sediment and biological samples, and large environmental datasets using reproducible computational workflows.

Simulation

Modeling the Earth system

Projects span climate models, geodynamic simulations, machine-learning emulators, and explainable AI for coastal hazards, including emerging AI methods for weather and climate prediction.

Technology

Access to the R/V F. G. Walton Smith, the SUSTAIN hurricane-scale wind-wave-surge laboratory, the CSTARS remote-sensing facility, genomics infrastructure, and HPC through UM's Frost Institute for Data Science and Computing.

Research Themes & Mentor Projects

Rather than organize the program by department, COAST integrates projects through four cross-cutting themes that together span the coupled coastal Earth system. The full three-year pool comprises 16 mentor projects (four per theme); no more than 10 are active in any single summer, matched to the cohort of 8–10 students. Many projects bridge two or more themes, and these cross-theme links are surfaced explicitly within each project.

1

Climate, Hazards, and Resilience

ATM · EVR · MGS · MBE

How climate variability and extreme events propagate through coastal atmosphere, solid Earth, and biological systems, and the adaptation responses of communities living with these hazards. South Florida serves as a shared observational laboratory across the four projects.

P. Zuidema ATM

Florida Atmospheric Boundary Layer Experiment (FABLE): vertical profiles of temperature, humidity, winds, and aerosols along a southeast Florida transect. Students apply quality control, daily-cycle compositing, and visualization to a curated profile subset.

Bridges to: T4 (observational technology and atmospheric measurement systems).

K. Mach EVR

Climate risk and adaptation science in partnership with local governments and community organizations. Students translate chronic-heat datasets into decision-relevant community metrics or evaluate flood-model output for one adaptation scenario.

Bridges to: T2 (community health, heat exposure, and human-environment vulnerability).

G. Lin MGS

Regional dense nodal seismic array across south Florida investigating shallow carbonate-platform structure and ocean-storm-solid-Earth coupling through the microseism wavefield. Students apply ambient-noise cross-correlation to a defined station subset.

Bridges to: T4 (dense-node seismic array as autonomous observing technology).

R. Heuer MBE

Effects of climate-related stressors and environmental pollutants on neural and behavioral function in Aplysia or zebrafish using behavioral assays, imaging, and physiology. Students conduct one focused experimental comparison.

Bridges to: T2 (organismal ecology and ecosystem-health implications) and T3 (pollutant biogeochemistry).

2

Coastal Ecosystems, Restoration, and Health

MBE · EVR · OCE

Coastal ecology, restoration biology, population biology, and the human-environment connections that link coastal ecosystems to community well-being. Three projects work within the Biscayne Bay and south Florida reef-tract system; the fourth supplies the open-ocean biogeochemical boundary condition for the same coastal system.

D. Lirman MBE

Coral-reef restoration research integrating field, laboratory, and citizen-science components. Students analyze a monitoring dataset or conduct a small experiment focused on one restoration-performance metric (outplant survival, growth, or thermal tolerance).

Bridges to: T1 (reef resilience under marine heatwaves and climate stressors).

D. Crawford & M. Oleksiak MBE

Population genomics of fish or invertebrate species in Biscayne Bay and adjacent south Florida waters, spanning DNA extraction, sequencing preparation, and computational analysis of one environmental contrast.

Bridges to: T4 (computational genomics pipelines and bioinformatics).

K. Ashad-Bishop EVR

Mixed-methods environmental-health research at the intersection of urban fishing, extreme heat, and medical vulnerability in Miami-Dade County. Students pursue qualitative analysis of urban-fisher interviews or analyze biometric and heat-sensor data with UM Miller School clinic partners.

Bridges to: T1 (extreme heat as hazard and community-resilience challenge).

M. Bif OCE

Ocean carbon and nitrogen cycling investigated using BGC-Argo floats, satellite remote sensing, and shipboard observations. Students analyze one target region and biogeochemical variable.

Bridges to: T3 (biogeochemical cycling) and T4 (autonomous floats and remote-sensing observing systems).

3

Biogeochemistry and Environmental Change

OCE · MBE · MGS

Coastal and open-ocean biogeochemical cycling, microbial-driven food webs, and the sediment and isotopic archives that record past and present environmental change. Each project reduces a defined material (particulate, tissue, sediment core, sequencing dataset, or fisheries record) to a focused measurement and interprets it within a longer environmental context.

H. Close OCE

Organic and isotopic analyses of marine particulate matter, sediments, or organism tissues to investigate carbon and nitrogen sourcing and food-web structure within the ocean biological pump.

Bridges to: T2 (food-web ecology and trophic structure).

M. Moniruzzaman MBE

Giant-virus impacts on coastal microbial food webs and biogeochemical cycling using field sampling, metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and bioinformatics. Students annotate a defined viral marker group across one environmental contrast.

Bridges to: T2 (microbial ecology and coastal food-web structure) and T4 (metagenomic and bioinformatic workflows).

G. Eberli, A. Oehlert, R. Weger MGS

High-recovery Last-Glacial-Maximum fringing-reef sediment-core archive offshore Mozambique (with J. Klaus and S. Goldberg). Students integrate petrophysical, mineralogical, or geochemical measurements into a deglacial sea-level framework.

Bridges to: T1 (paleo record of past sea-level hazard).

E. Babcock MBE

Quantitative marine ecology applied to fisheries and ecosystem management: bycatch of protected species, species-distribution modeling, food-web responses to nutrient pollution in Biscayne Bay, and seagrass-restoration value for juvenile gag-grouper production. Students pair with a Babcock-lab PhD student on one defined sub-question using version-controlled R and AI-assisted workflows.

Bridges to: T2 (ecosystem management and seagrass-restoration value for fisheries) and T4 (statistical modeling and AI-assisted analytical workflows).

4

Modeling, AI, and Technology

ATM · MGS · OCE

Computational modeling, machine learning, AI-assisted analysis, and observational technology as central scientific contributions across atmospheric, ocean, and solid-Earth systems. Students treat computation as a primary scientific instrument while connecting their work to observational and process projects elsewhere in the cohort.

S. Wang ATM

Neural-network emulator of the E-AIM aerosol thermodynamics model for integration into atmospheric-modeling workflows. Students train and benchmark the emulator across a defined aerosol-composition range and evaluate performance against E-AIM outputs.

Bridges to: T1 (aerosol-climate coupling).

M. Arcodia ATM

Explainable AI for prediction of coastal marine heatwaves in the Caribbean-Florida-Gulf region using climate-model output and reanalysis data. Students identify the dominant physical predictors driving model performance.

Bridges to: T1 (marine heatwave hazards) and T2 (downstream consequences for coastal ecosystems and fisheries).

A. Holt MGS

Computational geodynamics of double-subduction systems and their role in producing the fastest tectonic plate speeds on Earth. Students use global plate-reconstruction tools to test plate-speed scaling relationships against published numerical models.

F. P. Tuchen OCE

Multidecadal changes in equatorial Atlantic ocean currents from long-term moored observations, with possible research-cruise participation. Students conduct quality-controlled time-series analysis and evaluate climate-index relationships.

Bridges to: T1 (multidecadal climate variability) and T3 (long-term oceanographic record of environmental change).

How to Apply

Applications are received through the NSF Education & Training Application (ETAP) platform. There is no application fee. Prior research experience is not required: COAST actively welcomes earlier-stage undergraduates, including rising sophomores and community-college transfer students.

Confirm eligibility

U.S. citizens, nationals, or permanent residents currently enrolled in an undergraduate degree program. Students at all stages are encouraged to apply.

Prepare your materials

Personal statement, academic transcript (unofficial accepted), one faculty reference, and your ranked preferences among the COAST mentor projects.

Submit through NSF ETAP

All applications are managed through the NSF ETAP portal. Submitting through ETAP also supports longitudinal tracking of REU participants over time.

Project matching

The PI, Co-PI, Coordinator, and a rotating mentor subcommittee review applications using a structured rubric. Final offers and project matches are made in late March, with alternates identified.

Arrive on Virginia Key

Cohort programming begins in June with an orientation week and a campus tour of Rosenstiel and CIMAS.

Eligibility & Selection Criteria

Per NSF requirements, applicants must be U.S. citizens, nationals, or permanent residents enrolled in an undergraduate degree program at the time of the summer experience. Students who will graduate before the program starts are not eligible.

Selection emphasizes:

  • Motivation and curiosity
  • Preparation appropriate to the proposed project
  • Potential for growth as a researcher
  • Alignment with available mentor projects
  • Evidence of persistence, problem-solving, or teamwork

At least half of each cohort is drawn from academic institutions where STEM research opportunities are limited, including primarily undergraduate institutions, community colleges, and minority-serving institutions. We actively encourage applications from students at these institutions.

Student Life & Logistics

COAST is designed as a fully supported in-person experience. Stipend, housing, meals, and round-trip travel to Miami are all covered. Students live together on the University of Miami's Coral Gables campus and commute by UM inter-campus shuttle to the Rosenstiel campus on Virginia Key.

$

Stipend & Support

Participants receive a competitive weekly stipend across all ten weeks of the program (set at the NSF-expected level), plus a meal per-diem and reimbursement for round-trip travel to Miami. There is no cost to participate.

⌂

Housing

Shared cohort housing is provided on the University of Miami's Coral Gables campus for the full ten weeks. Living together strengthens cohort cohesion and removes financial and logistical barriers for students from outside the Miami area.

↻

Getting around

Free UM inter-campus transportation runs between Coral Gables and the Rosenstiel campus on Virginia Key. Cohort field activities (CSTARS, the R/V Walton Smith, the National Hurricane Center, the Florida Reef Tract, and the Everglades) are arranged by the program.

☷

Research Environment

You'll have access to the R/V F. G. Walton Smith, the SUSTAIN hurricane-scale wind-wave-surge laboratory, CSTARS remote sensing, genomics and sequencing facilities, and high-performance computing through UM's Frost Institute for Data Science and Computing.

⚙

Cohort Programming

Weekly research seminars, cross-project integration discussions, skills workshops (Python/Jupyter, version control, scientific writing, oral presentation), Responsible Conduct of Research training, career-pathways panels, and a culminating end-of-summer public symposium.

⚒

Career Pathways

Career panels span academia, NOAA and other federal laboratories, industry, non-profit, and applied-science paths. Selected students are supported to present at national meetings (AGU, AMS, Ocean Sciences, ASLO, SACNAS) and to continue research with their mentor beyond the summer.

Living in Miami

The Rosenstiel campus on Virginia Key sits between Biscayne Bay and the Atlantic, a short causeway ride from downtown Miami and Miami Beach. Summer in South Florida is hot, humid, and shaped by sea-breeze convection and the seasonal threat of tropical cyclones; students experience first-hand the demanding environmental conditions they study. Miami is one of the most demographically diverse coastal cities in the United States, with rich Cuban, Haitian, Caribbean, Central American, and South American communities and a vibrant arts, music, and food scene.

Safe, Respectful, and Inclusive Environment

COAST is committed to a safe, respectful, inclusive, and harassment-free environment for all participants. Orientation week includes review of UM policies and reporting procedures, expectations of professional conduct, and field-safety planning. The Site Coordinator works with the UM Office of Disability Services to provide individualized accommodations for students with disabilities, including accessible housing, transportation, and lab and field accommodations.
