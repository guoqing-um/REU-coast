Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to common questions about eligibility, support, dates, and applying.

U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, or U.S. permanent residents enrolled in an associate or baccalaureate degree program at the time of the summer experience, including eligible transfer and incoming students. Students who will have completed a bachelor's degree before the program begins are not eligible.

No. Most COAST participants arrive with classroom experience and no research of their own. Selection emphasizes motivation, curiosity, and potential for growth, alongside preparation appropriate to the project you rank. Rising sophomores and juniors and community-college transfer students are especially encouraged to apply.

The NSF stipend of $700 per week across all ten weeks, program-arranged shared cohort housing, and round-trip travel to and from Miami. The stipend covers remaining day-to-day living costs including meals and daily transit, so there is no separate meal allowance. There is no cost to participate.

COAST leases and pays for shared cohort housing centrally, so you never have to find a place or front the cost. Rosenstiel occupies its own island campus on Virginia Key, which has essentially no residential housing, so the cohort is housed together in shared near-campus units on the mainland or Key Biscayne, along the Metrobus Route 26 corridor. Route 26 runs directly to campus.

Ten weeks each summer, roughly mid-May through late July, opening with an orientation week and closing with a research symposium where you present your work. Year 1 is Summer 2028, followed by Summers 2029 and 2030. Exact dates will be confirmed upon NSF award.

Through the NSF Education & Training Application (ETAP) platform. There is no application fee. You will provide a personal statement, a transcript (unofficial accepted), one faculty reference, and your ranked preferences among the COAST projects. For the Summer 2028 cohort, applications open in October 2027 and close in late January 2028, with offers in late March. See the How to Apply page.

The program team matches students to projects on your ranked preferences, your preparation, and mentor availability. Up to ten of the sixteen projects run in any given summer.

Projects span three science domains: Coastal Hazards and Climate Extremes; Living Coasts: Ecosystems, Organisms, and Human Health; and Coastal Change and Earth-System Cycles, with a cross-cutting Modeling, AI, and Technology layer running through all three. Work ranges from field and laboratory observation to population genomics, isotope geochemistry, seismology, climate modeling, and explainable AI. See the Research page.

A reproducible analysis, a curated dataset, a written report, and a presentation at the Week-10 symposium. Many participants go on to a conference abstract, a thesis chapter, a co-authored manuscript, or a fellowship application, with mentor support into the academic year.

Hotter and more humid than most applicants are used to, and the program plans for it. Laboratory and cohort spaces are air-conditioned, field activities are scheduled for early morning or late afternoon, cooling and sun-protection gear is provided, and heat-safety training is part of Week-1 orientation.

Yes. COAST works with the UM Office of Disability Services to arrange individual accommodations, including accessible housing, transportation, and lab and field accommodations. Just let us know what you need.
